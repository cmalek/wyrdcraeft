Sound Terms for Historical Linguistics

A short working glossary for the sound labels that appear all the time in historical grammar, even when your main goal is reading Old English rather than studying phonetics for its own sake.

Core Types

TermPlain meaningWhy you keep seeing it
VowelA sound with relatively open airflow.Many sound changes target stressed versus unstressed vowels, front versus back vowels, or long versus short vowels.
ConsonantA sound with greater obstruction in the mouth.Most named laws describe changes in consonant classes.
StopA consonant with full closure and release.Grimm's Law is often described in terms of stops shifting.
FricativeA consonant with friction from narrow airflow.Very common in Germanic sound laws: f, þ, h, s.
SibilantA hissy or hushing fricative.Useful when reading discussions of s-like sounds and their behavior.
NasalA sound with airflow through the nose.Often relevant in assimilation and cluster changes.
LiquidTraditional class including l and r.Liquids often trigger or block nearby vowel changes.
GlideA very vowel-like consonant, especially j and w.Important for i-mutation and other palatal or labiovelar developments. In Proto-Germanic and Gothic, j is usually the y-sound [j], like the start of English yes or German ja.

Voicing and Breath

TermPlain meaningRough cue
VoicedThe vocal folds vibrate.Put a hand on your throat for z or d.
VoicelessThe vocal folds do not vibrate.Compare s or t.
AspiratedReleased with a noticeable puff of breath.English p in pin is more aspirated than p in spin.
UnaspiratedReleased without that strong puff.Common contrast in phonetic descriptions of older stop systems.

Place Words

TermPlain meaningExample area
LabialMade with the lips.p, b, m, w
DentalMade at or near the teeth.þ, ð
AlveolarMade at the ridge behind the teeth.t, d, n, s, l, r
VelarMade with the back of the tongue against the soft palate.k, g
PalatalMade with the tongue toward the hard palate.j, or palatalized consonants
PalatalizedStill basically another consonant, but pronounced with a secondary y-like coloring or movement toward the palate.Useful for understanding OE ċ, ġ, and many historical front-vowel effects.

Change Words

TermPlain meaning
AblautAn inherited vowel alternation inside the same word-family or paradigm, not caused by a nearby sound in the current form. Strong-verb patterns and pairs like fōt beside the older background of fēt are where you keep meeting it.
AssimilationOne sound becomes more like a nearby sound.
DissimilationOne sound becomes less like a nearby sound.
SyncopeLoss of an unstressed vowel inside a word.
ApocopeLoss of a final sound or syllable.
GeminationConsonant lengthening.
Lenite / lenitionTo weaken a sound, or the process of weakening. A stop may become a fricative, a fricative may become weaker still, or a consonant may disappear altogether.
LenitionA sound becomes weaker or less obstructed.
FortitionA sound becomes stronger or more obstructed.
Mutation / umlautA vowel changes because of a vowel or glide in the next syllable. Often that trigger belonged to an older ending that later disappeared, which is why attested pairs like mann ~ menn and fōt ~ fēt can look puzzling at first. In this context j means the glide , while i is the high front vowel .
PalatalizationA consonant shifts toward a palatal or palatalized pronunciation, often because of a nearby front vowel or j.

Two Reading Tips

  1. If a historical explanation feels opaque, ask first whether the hard word is naming a sound class, a place of articulation, or a type of change.
  2. In older philological writing, terms may be a little traditional or broad. The goal is fast recognition, not perfect modern phonetics on day one.
