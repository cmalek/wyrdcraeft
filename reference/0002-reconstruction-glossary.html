Reconstruction Glossary

The minimum vocabulary you need to read historical-linguistics explanations without getting buried.

TermPlain meaning
Reconstructed formA form not directly attested in writing, inferred from evidence. It is written with an asterisk, like *fōtiz.
Proto-languageThe common ancestor of a language family or subgroup, such as Proto-Germanic.
ReflexA later form descended from an earlier one. Old English fōt is a reflex of an earlier Germanic form, and Modern English foot is a reflex of Old English fōt.
CognateA related word inherited from a shared ancestor, not just a similar-looking word.
Sound correspondenceA recurring match across related languages, such as English f corresponding to Latin p.
Comparative methodThe main method for reconstructing unattested ancestors by comparing related languages and finding regular correspondences.
Internal reconstructionUsing irregularities inside one language to infer an earlier stage of that same language.
AblautAn inherited vowel alternation inside the same word-family or paradigm. It is not caused by a neighboring sound in the current form. Good intuition examples are PIE *ped- beside *pod-, and English strong-verb patterns like sing / sang / sung.
Umlaut / mutationA later sound change where one vowel is altered because of a vowel or glide in the next syllable. In Old English, a classic example is fōt beside plural fēt, where the later i-sound in the ending triggered the fronted vowel.
Ablaut gradeOne member of an inherited vowel-alternation pattern, such as e-grade, o-grade, or zero-grade. So o-grade vocalism means the form uses the o-vowel version of that older pattern. A classic example is the "foot" family: compare PIE *ped--type e-grade, *pod--type o-grade, and zero-grade forms where the root vowel disappears. In later Indo-European languages you keep meeting these inherited alternations in paradigms and word-families rather than as brand-new sound changes.
AnalogyReshaping by pattern pressure, often making a form more regular or extending an existing pattern. A modern English example is bring / brang / brung modeled on sing / sang / sung.
LevelingA kind of analogy where differences inside a paradigm are reduced so the forms become more alike. A simple intuition is when an older pattern with alternations gets smoothed out because speakers prefer one form to spread across the set.
Sound lawA regular sound change applying across a class of forms under stated conditions.

Quick ablaut intuition: if e-grade is the version with e, and o-grade is the version with o, then zero-grade is the version where that vowel disappears. A very rough schematic example is *bher- (e-grade), *bhor- (o-grade), and *bhr- (zero-grade). In old verb systems, English still gives you a faint feel for this kind of inherited alternation in patterns like sing / sang / sung, even though the full PIE system is older and more complex.

Quick contrast: ablaut is an old inherited pattern inside a family of forms, while umlaut is a later triggered sound change caused by a nearby vowel or glide. A compact Old English way to remember this is: sing / sang / sung feels like ablaut, but fōt / fēt is the classic umlaut example.

Quick leveling intuition: suppose older sound history leaves a verb with alternating consonants in different forms. If later speakers reduce that variation and let one pattern spread, that is leveling. A modern English-style comparison is when speakers regularize an irregular set, as with dive / dived instead of older competing patterns like dove.
