Input Quality and Page Preparation

This lesson is about improving your odds before OCR even starts: choosing a good source, deciding when to split pages, and applying only the preparation that actually helps the recognizer.

Win for today: by the end, you should be able to decide whether a source is good enough for first-pass OCR, whether a page should stay whole or be split into smaller regions, and which preprocessing steps are worth trying first.

1. Start With The Best Source You Can Get

The cheapest OCR improvement is often not an image-processing trick. It is finding a better input witness. If you have three candidate PDFs of the same book, compare the source quality before you tune the recognizer.

If a source has...Prefer it?Why
high-resolution page images with stable contrastyessmall glyph details and diacritics survive better
crooked pages, warped lines, or shadowed guttersno if a better witness existsyou will spend your whole pipeline compensating for geometry problems
already-compressed JPEG artifacts or smeared textno if avoidablethe recognizer loses stroke shape before it even sees the page
clear typography but weak metadataoften yesOCR usually cares more about pixels than catalog quality
a trustworthy corrected text witness alongside the scansyes, as supportthat witness can become your gold or alignment aid later

Rule of thumb: prefer the best visual witness for OCR, even if a worse-looking source is easier to download or already bundled into another workflow.

2. Diagnose The Page Before You Preprocess It

Before changing contrast or slicing the page, identify the real bottleneck. First-pass OCR quality usually fails because of one or more of these:

  • text is too small relative to the model's effective input resolution
  • columns or tables confuse reading order
  • the page is skewed, warped, or shadowed
  • contrast is weak enough that strokes disappear into the background
  • typographic distinctions like bold and italics are too subtle at page scale
  • the page contains more text density than one model input can handle cleanly

Do not preprocess blindly. Each transformation can help one failure mode while damaging another.

3. The Preparation Ladder

Try the least destructive move that solves the problem.

Better source first: swap in a cleaner scan if possible.
Geometry fixes second: crop borders, deskew, and dewarp before you do heavy tonal changes.
Contrast and denoising third: increase legibility only as much as needed to preserve strokes.
Region splitting fourth: split a page only when scale or layout is the real bottleneck.
Aggressive transforms last: hard binarization, morphology, or extreme sharpening only when simpler steps still fail.

5. When To Split A Page

Splitting is appropriate when the model cannot devote enough effective resolution to each line or column on the full page. This often happens with dictionaries, glossaries, and grammars in small type.

Split when: the page is dense, the text is small, the layout has narrow columns, or typographic distinctions disappear at full-page scale.

Do not split just because you can: every split creates a reassembly problem, and bad reassembly can destroy reading order or table structure.

6. Bosworth-Toller As A Case Study

anglosaxondictio00bosw.pdf is a good example of a page that benefits from splitting. The pages are densely packed, the font is small, and the text is laid out in two columns. At full-page scale, each column can become too small relative to the OCR model's usable input budget.

The strategy you described is exactly the kind of thing to try:

  • split each page into four images
  • make each tile's longest side match the model's preferred maximum input dimension
  • run OCR on each tile separately
  • reassemble in a fixed reading order

Why this helps:

  • each line of text occupies more pixels
  • small diacritics and ligatures are less likely to vanish
  • column order becomes easier to control
  • bold/italic or other style differences may survive better if the glyphs are rendered larger

What you must guard against:

  • tiles cutting through lines or entries awkwardly
  • reassembly in the wrong reading order
  • duplicate text in overlapping regions
  • tables or marginal structures no longer matching the prose assumptions

6a. One Model Or Two Stages?

If you care about both hard-text recognition and typographic structure, do not assume one OCR model will do both equally well.

ApproachStrengthWeakness
single OCR modelsimpler pipeline, fewer moving partsthe best text recognizer may strip or flatten bold, italics, and font distinctions
two-stage pipelinelets you optimize text recognition and style/layout separatelyyou must align the outputs back together

Good practical rule: if the typography carries meaning, treat style as its own evidence layer. Let one system focus on producing the best witness text, and let another system detect style/layout spans such as bold, italic, or region classes.

This matters for sources like Wright and Bosworth-Toller. A model that gives excellent readable text may still discard the distinction between bold Old English headwords and italic modern-language glosses. In that case the right question is not “which one model is perfect?” but “how do I combine the best text witness with the best style/layout witness?”

A strong pattern is:

Stage 1: run the OCR engine that gives the best hard-text recognition on difficult pages.
Stage 2: run a layout or document-analysis system that preserves spans, regions, or font properties.
Stage 3: align style spans back onto the OCR witness.
Stage 4: validate text fidelity and style retention separately.

7. Contrast, Binarization, and Other Common Temptations

People often jump straight to “increase contrast” or “make it black and white.” Sometimes that helps. Sometimes it ruins the very features you care about.

TransformationCan helpCan hurt
contrast boostfaint text on gray paperthin macrons, breathings, or punctuation can blow out
binarizationclear dark text with noisy backgroundsubtle stroke width and typographic differences may disappear
sharpeningsoft scanshalos can make glyph shapes less realistic
erosion / dilationspecial cases of broken or swollen strokesvery easy to destroy ligatures and diacritics

Safe default: keep the first pass as close to the source image as possible, with only the minimum geometry and tonal cleanup needed to make the page legible. Heavy transforms are worth trying only when you can measure that they helped.

8. What A Good First-Pass Experiment Looks Like

For one difficult source, compare a few variants rather than guessing:

Variant A: whole page, minimal cleanup.
Variant B: whole page, gentle deskew and contrast normalization.
Variant C: split by column or tile, same cleanup.
Variant D: split plus any extra transformation you suspect helps, such as denoising.

Then compare them using the validation approach from Lesson 0004. For small-font dictionary pages, the winner is often not the cleanest-looking image to human eyes, but the one that best preserves the exact graphemes and structure you care about.
