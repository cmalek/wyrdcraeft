Lossless OCR Pipeline for Difficult PDFs

This lesson gives you the core mental model for hard philological PDFs: generate witnesses first, measure quality early, and only then derive structure for software or agents.

Win for today: by the end, you should be able to sketch a seven-stage OCR-to-structured-data workflow and say where raw witnesses, overlays, and normalized exports each belong.

1. The Big Idea

If a PDF is image-only and full of diacritics, Greek, tables, and philological symbols, the first mistake to avoid is treating OCR text as already-correct text. OCR output is a witness: useful, valuable, and often surprisingly good, but still something that must remain traceable back to the page image.

Use this sentence until it feels obvious: OCR produces evidence; structured data produces claims. Evidence should be preserved. Claims should point back to evidence.

2. The Seven Stages

StageQuestion it answersTypical artifact
AcquireWhat exact pages are we transcribing?source PDF or page images

3. How To Split The Work

OCR engine: use a strong recognizer for the first pass, especially one that preserves reading order and tolerates tables, equations, and mixed layouts. Modern VLM-based systems such as olmocr are often better than classic OCR alone on messy scholarly PDFs.
Layout-bearing fallback artifacts: when you need coordinates or line-level evidence, keep formats like hOCR, TSV, ALTO, or PAGE XML instead of relying only on cleaned plain text.
Case bundle or review packet: for hard structured-data work, keep witnesses, anchors, raw fragments, review decisions, and derived normalized output side by side so nothing important becomes invisible.

This is the lazy but correct split: one layer produces OCR artifacts, another layer evaluates them, and a later layer turns the trustworthy parts into shareable structure.

4. Minimal Human Involvement Does Not Mean Zero Review

The durable goal is not "never look at the text." The durable goal is review by exception. Let the machine do the bulk work, then spend human attention only on uncertain spans, damaged characters, broken table order, or places where the structured claim would otherwise outrun the evidence.

Automate hardKeep human for
page prep, OCR, normalization, scoring, bundle assemblyambiguous characters, fragment splits, table interpretation, hard symbols
metrics for CER, WER, thorn, eth, and macron preservationaccept/reject decisions on flagged exceptions
export of machine-usable JSON/YAMLfinal trust decisions on the uncertain tail

5. The Three Layers You Must Not Confuse

Raw witness: what the OCR system said.

Overlay: what a human or later pass decided to correct.

Normalized export: what downstream software or agents should consume.

If you collapse these into one file, you lose the ability to audit mistakes, improve the pipeline, or compare one OCR run against another. A good practical rule is to keep one raw layer, one correction layer, and one derived-consumer layer even if your exact filenames differ.

6. What Quality Gates Matter Here

On difficult Old English material, a generic "looks readable" check is too weak. Historical OCR research and practical workflow guides both point toward evaluation that is specific to the document and to the downstream use case, not just a single coarse score.

FeatureWhy it mattersGate idea
thorn / eththey change lexical identity and morphologytrack preservation and thorn-to-p rate
macronsthey matter for philology, lookup, and normalizationtrack macron recall separately
Greek / symbolsthey often encode grammatical or comparative meaningsample-check against witness images

Important warning from the post-correction literature: a system can lower CER and still make the text less trustworthy by silently normalizing, modernizing, or hallucinating. So your gates should ask both "Did the text get cleaner?" and "Did we preserve the characters, symbols, and uncertainty that matter?"

6a. How To Define Good Metrics

Start from the kinds of mistakes that would actually hurt your downstream use. For philological PDFs, that usually means not one score but a small bundle of scores, each tied to one failure family. A good metric suite should tell you what broke, not just that the page got a worse number.

Failure familyWhat to measureWhy it matters
Diacritic preservationrecall for macrons and other marks, plus confusion countsā, ī̆, ū̆, ό, and ā̃ are not decoration; losing them destroys evidence
Character-substitution driftconfusion matrix for philological and Greek charactersη α ρ ι χ ν becoming n a p i x v is a different class of error from ordinary OCR noise
Normalization driftcounts of expansions and substitutions such as æ → ae or macron-to-umlautthe text may look readable while still no longer being diplomatically faithful
Line-join fidelityprecision and recall for dehyphenation at line breakssome split words should be rejoined, but real hyphenated forms should not be flattened blindly
Table fidelitycell coverage, row/column order, and reading-order accuracytable OCR can have decent word accuracy while still destroying the structure you care about
Typographic signal retentionretention of bold, italics, size/weight classes, or at least tagged spans for themfont distinctions may carry real structure, such as lemma vs. gloss or Old English vs. Modern English definition

Good default rule: define one general text score, then add a few targeted philology scores. For example: CER or WER for overall OCR quality, diacritic recall for marked vowels, a character-confusion report for Greek and special symbols, dehyphenation accuracy for line endings, and a table-structure score for paradigm pages.

6c. What To Do With Wright-Like Documents

A document like WrightAndWright.pdf is a good reminder that validation should be document-aware. If bold Old English headwords and italic Modern English glosses carry structure, then style retention is not a cosmetic metric. It is part of whether the OCR witness preserved information that later parsing could use.

For documents like this, define a watchlist before you run experiments: macronized vowels, ligatures like æ, Greek letters, rare diacritics, line-break joins, table pages, and style-marked spans. Then require every OCR run to report those categories separately instead of hiding them inside one average score.

6d. A Practical Metric Stack

Layer 1: overall CER or WER for a coarse sanity check.
Layer 2: philology-sensitive character metrics for macrons, ligatures, thorn/eth, Greek, and rare diacritics.
Layer 3: structure metrics for line joins, tables, and reading order.
Layer 4: formatting or style-retention metrics when typography encodes meaning.
Layer 5: exception flags that force review even when the averages look acceptable.

The main idea is simple: measure exactly the kinds of damage you cannot afford to miss. If a failure mode matters to later parsing, translation, or scholarly trust, it deserves its own metric.

6e. Walkthrough: Designing A Macron Metric On Wright Page 26

The natural question is: how do I measure recall without just eyeballing the whole page every time? The answer is: you use your eyes once to create a small gold reference, and after that the metric runs automatically.

Why this page works well: PDF page 26 of WrightAndWright.pdf (printed page 6 in the Phonology section) is rich in long vowels and also carries other useful signal: æ, ǣ, þ, bold Old English forms, italic Modern English glosses, and several places where script and typography matter to later structure.

Step 1: Choose The Exact Thing You Want Recall To Mean

For this page, do not define recall as “page looked mostly right.” Define it narrowly:

Macron recall = number of expected macron-bearing graphemes preserved correctly in the OCR witness, divided by the number of macron-bearing graphemes in the gold transcription.

That means you are checking grapheme occurrences like ā, ǣ, ē, ī, ō, and ū, not just whether the surrounding word is “close enough.”

Important distinction: if you want true recall, you need an expected count from a gold reference. Without that, you can still do anomaly detection or heuristic estimation, but you cannot honestly claim exact recall. The practical compromise is micro-gold: correct only the phenomena you care about on a few calibration pages, then score future OCR runs automatically against those.

Step 2: Build A Small Gold Slice Once

Pick a bounded region from the page and transcribe it carefully by hand one time. For this page, you already did that. Your corrected text is exactly the kind of gold slice we want.

Gold excerpt from your corrected page:

*land*; **lang**, **long**, *long*; **mann**, **monn**, *man*; **nama**, **noma**, *name*. See § 59.

**ā** had the same sound as the **a** in NE. **father**, as **ān**, *one*; **bān**, *bone*; **rāp**, *rope*; **twā**, *two*; **cnāwan**, *to know*; **māwan**, *to mow*; **sāwan**, *to sow*.

**æ** had the same sound as the **a** in NE. hat, as **dæg**, *day*; **fæder**, *father*; **fæstan**, *to fasten*; **hæfde**, *he had*; **mægden**, *maiden*.

**ǣ** had the same sound as the **ai** in NE. air, and the **è** in French **père**, as **dǣd**, *deed*; **sǣd**, *seed*; **wǣpen**, *weapon*; **clǣne**, *clean*; **hǣlan**, *to heal*; **lǣdan**, *to lead*; **sǣ**, *sea*.

**ē** had the same sound as the **e** in NHG. reh, as **hēr**, *here*; **cwēn**, *queen*; **fēdan**, *to feed*; **grēne**, *green*; **tēþ**, *teeth*.

**ī** had the same sound as the **i** in NHG. ihn, and nearly the same sound as the ee in NE. feed, as **līf**, *life*; **mīn**, *my*; **tīd**, *time*; **fīf**, *five*; **sīþe**, *scythe*.

**ō** had the same sound as the **o** in NHG. bote, as **brōþor**, *brother*; **grōwan**, *to grow*; **mōdor**, *mother*; **mōna**, *moon*; **sōna**, *soon*; **gōs**, *goose*; **ōþer**, *other*; **þōhte**, *he thought*.

**ū** had the same sound as the **u** in NHG. gut, and nearly the same sound as the oo in NE. food, as **cū**, *cow*; **hūs**, *house*; **sūr**, *sour*; **ūt**, *out*; **mūþ**, *mouth*; **ūs**, *us*.

This gives you several things at once:

  • the exact Unicode graphemes
  • macron-bearing forms such as ān, bān, hēr, līf, brōþor, and mūþ
  • ligatures like æ and ǣ
  • thorn in forms like tēþ, sīþe, brōþor, and þōhte
  • style markers for bold Old English and italic Modern English glosses

After that one careful transcription, every future OCR candidate can be compared against the same gold automatically.

Step 4: Compute The Metric Automatically

Now you can count the expected targets from the gold transcription rather than from guesswork. Suppose your gold slice contains 40 macron-bearing grapheme occurrences. If the OCR witness preserves 36 of them exactly, drops 3, and changes 1 into an umlauted form, then:

macron_recall = 36 / 40 = 0.90 = 90%

Do not stop there. Also record the confusion breakdown, because lost macron and wrong diacritic substituted are different failure modes:

  • macron_lost = 3
  • macron_to_umlaut = 1
  • wrong_base_letter_with_mark = 0

Step 5: Set A Floor From Review Cost, Not Vibes

A “floor” is the lowest score you will still accept before the page or run is kicked into manual review. The easiest way to choose one is to connect it to how many fixes a human would have to make.

Example rule: on a validation slice of about 40 macron-bearing graphemes, allow at most 2 macron failures. That implies a floor of 38 / 40 = 95% macron recall.

Why this is better than guessing:

  • it ties the threshold to visible correction burden
  • it scales to larger samples the same way
  • it makes the review rule explicit: “more than two macron repairs on this slice is too much”

You can do the same for the other signals on this page:

  • ligature floor: maybe zero tolerated æ → ae expansions on the validation slice if ligature fidelity is critical
  • thorn floor: maybe zero tolerated þ → p substitutions because the confusion is too damaging
  • style floor: require that bold Old English forms and italic Modern English glosses are retained above some minimum span-level recall if you plan to parse those distinctions later

Step 5a: What If You Do Not Yet Have A Gold Count?

Then be explicit that you are doing something weaker than recall.

What you haveWhat you can claimWhat you cannot claim
hand-corrected page or slicetrue recall and exact confusion countsnothing special; this is real ground truth
trusted corrected edition or parallel textapproximate recall after alignmentperfect page-faithful counts without checking alignment errors
only OCR outputs and heuristicsanomaly rates, disagreement rates, suspicious-substitution countstrue recall

So the honest workflow is:

Micro-gold: correct a few representative pages like this one.
Calibrate: use those pages to set thresholds for macrons, ligatures, thorn, and style.
Scale: use cheaper anomaly metrics on the rest of the corpus to decide what needs review.

Recommended startup workflow: do not begin by trying to gold the whole book. Start with one calibration page, build real metrics for that page, iterate the OCR pipeline until the scores and correction burden are acceptable, then expand to a small multi-page calibration set. Only after that should you roll the workflow out to the broader corpus.

Step 6: Use More Than One Slice Before You Freeze The Threshold

Do not set the permanent floor from one page alone. Use page 26 as your first calibration slice, then add a few more pages with different difficulty profiles: one table-heavy page, one page with Greek, one page with rare diacritics, one page with many line-break joins. Run your OCR candidates across all of them and ask:

  • what score range do the acceptable runs actually achieve?
  • at what score does manual cleanup become annoying enough to reject the run?
  • which failure types are rare but unacceptable, even when the average score looks fine?

That gives you a defensible floor. In practice, the workflow is:

Calibrate: build a few gold slices and measure several OCR candidates.
Inspect: compare the scores against actual correction burden.
Choose: set the floor just above the point where the witness becomes too expensive or too lossy to trust.
Separate: keep “average quality” thresholds distinct from “hard failure” thresholds like losing bold/italic structure or misreading Greek as Latin.

So the short answer is: you test recall with your eyes once to make a gold sample, then with code forever after. And you define the floor by deciding how much specific damage you are willing to repair by hand.
